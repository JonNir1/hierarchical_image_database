Background

The Project

We have 725 photographs of objects. We want to generate a similarity map over the image, to determine which images are too similar to be presented together in a visual search task. To create the map, we use Prolific to collect similarity ratings from online raters.
As a secondary goal, we want to check if the image preprocessing pipeline that is commonly applied on EEG/MRI experiments using the SHINE toolkit affects the perceptual similarity scores of the processed images.

The SpAM Task

The dataset consists of 725 images, which make 262,450 possible pairs. No single subject can rate that many pairs. Instead, we use the Spatial Arrangement Method: participants are presented with a subset of k images, and they are asked to place each image within a 2D canvas based on their similarity – similar images should be closer together, and dissimilar images are placed far apart. This way, perceptual similarity is inferred from the Euclidean distance between pairs of images, which can be collected much faster than the classical pairwise ratings using a Likert scale. A recent study (Robbins et al., 2025, experiment 2) reported correlations between the two methods ranging from r = 0.04 to 0.40 depending on category.

The two pairs below are the worked examples shown to participants during the instructions, before they begin. Each row is one arrangement: on the left, the images as the trial opens, scattered at random positions inside the canvas; on the right, the same images after they have been arranged by similarity. Note that these use the pictogram set reserved for the practice and catch trials, not the 725 experimental images, so that no experimental image is seen before its trial.

Image-to-Trial Allocation

With t=18 trials and k=20 unique images per trial, each (retained) participant covers 360 of the 725 images, and produces ½t·k(k-1) = 3420 distinct ratings (not including the repeated trials), which are 1.3% of all possible ratings. Importantly, the images are allocated randomly across subjects, which could lead to some images being over sampled while others are rarely seen, and many image-pairs never being judged at all. An alternative allocation method, introduced by MacDonald et al. (2020), guarantees an almost-optimally balanced allocation. However, it does not use strictly disjoint subsets of images across trials for the same participant – a criterion we require due to our repeatability analysis (repeated trials). Therefore, we created a constrained version of the MacDonald allocation algorithm, which forfeits some of the allocation-balance in order to guarantee a participant only sees images within a single context (i.e., no image appears in more than a single trial, except for repeated trials where the whole context is repeated).
While it is expected that the MacDonald algorithm (and our constrained version) would outperform the random assignment in pair-coverage and allocation balance, it is unclear whether the wider coverage translates to a worthwhile improvement in embedding stability, generalizability, or granularity.

Our Experiment

We collected data from Ntotal participants using Prolific. Each participant performed t=18 SpAM trials, each with k=20 unique images. Four repeat-trials – where participants needed to re-arrange a set of images they had already seen – were interleaved throughout the experiment. Those repeated measurements are used to assess participants' repeatability. Additionally, three catch-trials were presented randomly, requiring the participant to perform a slight variation of the task (e.g. "move all images to the bottom-right corner").
The first 8 trials (6 original, 2 repeats) were used to screen out subjects who do not perform the task: they move too-few items, their arrangement is too concentrated, or they fail to reproduce their own arrangements (Spearman R < 0).

Deriving the Perceptual Map

Generating the Embeddings

Each participant contributes a sparse set of pairwise distances. Pooling them across participants yields a single 725×725 dissimilarity matrix in which most cells remain empty. We turn that matrix into coordinates using Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) – specifically weighted SMACOF, which assigns zero weight to unobserved pairs so they neither constrain the solution nor need to be imputed.
Two properties of MDS matter for everything that follows. First, the dimensionality is an input, not an output: the algorithm embeds the images in however many dimensions it is given and returns a solution for any choice, so d must be selected on separate grounds (see Choosing the Dimensionality). Second, the graph of observed pairs must form a single connected component – if the images split into two subsets with no judged pair bridging them, their relative position is unconstrained and no unique solution exists. Connectivity is necessary but far from sufficient: a barely-connected graph still converges, but to an unstable solution that a second sample would not reproduce. Quantifying that gap is the purpose of Is the Map Any Good?.

Clustering the Embeddings

Grouping the images is the point of the exercise, so we cluster the recovered coordinates two ways, chosen because they fail differently. Agglomerative clustering merges the closest pairs of clusters upward into a dendrogram (we use average, ward and complete linkage), which is then cut at a chosen k. It assigns every image to some cluster, and the resulting partitions support Variation of Information, a true metric on partitions, so two cohorts' groupings can be compared on a scale that obeys the triangle inequality. Its weakness is exactly that completeness: an image resembling nothing else is still forced into a group. HDBSCAN is density-based and governed by min_cluster_size rather than by k; it discovers how many clusters there are and may leave an image unassigned, labelling it noise. That answers the question agglomerative clustering structurally cannot – "does this image belong to any group at all?" – which for stimulus construction is the useful answer, since an image resembling nothing else is the safest one to use. Its noise label breaks the metric properties, so it is run descriptively and never enters the VI chain.

Why Run These Simulations?

Three questions, none of which can be answered from the pilot alone, and all of which have to be settled before committing to a full data collection.

  1. Required Ntotal. How many participants must be recruited in order to retain N who pass screening and yield sufficiently stable embeddings.
  2. Sensitivity analysis at N = 50 and 75. Failing a definitive answer to (1), whether those specific sample sizes give embeddings stable and generalizable enough for the downstream claims we intend to make.
  3. Allocation-arm comparison. Random versus constrained-MacDonald, judged both on pair coverage and balance, and on the same stability and generalizability criteria as (2). Wider coverage is expected; whether it buys better embeddings is not.

From Embedding to Arrangement

A simulated participant is generated in three steps:

  1. Perspective – the d-dimensional ground-truth coordinates are re-weighted per subject, so different people effectively attend to different dimensions. The weights are drawn once per subject and stay fixed for their whole session.
  2. Projection – for each trial, the k=20 selected images are projected onto the two leading principal axes of the re-weighted cloud. This is the same lossy step a real participant performs when forced to express a high-dimensional judgement on a flat screen.
    Note what the re-weighting in step 1 buys here. Inflating a dimension inflates its variance, which promotes it into the top two: a subject weighting dimension 5 heavily sees a plane dominated by dimension 5, while another subject sees a different plane entirely. This is how the population samples all d dimensions rather than everyone viewing the two globally-largest ones. The projection itself involves no randomness – it is fully determined by the subject's weights and by which images share the trial.
  3. Placement – the projected points are fitted into a bounded rectangle and perturbed by placement noise. Distances are then read off the final arrangement exactly as the task records them.

The rectangle matters. Real arrangements are constrained by the canvas edges, and an unbounded model produced per-trial maximum distances of 1.39 on a scale whose ceiling is 1.0 – arrangements no participant could have made.

Noise Estimation

Variability enters the model at each of the three steps above, and the sources are distinct. Two are estimated from the pilot; the other two are structural, and would be present however the model were parameterised. Taking them in step order:

  • Across-subject noise (step i, estimated) – systematic differences in which dimensions a person weighs. This is not random error: each subject is internally consistent, they simply disagree about what matters. It is what makes two careful participants produce different arrangements, and it is estimated from between-subject agreement over the pairs they both judged (mean r=0.142, n=47).
  • Projection variability (step ii, not estimated) – the 2D plane is not fixed. It depends on the subject's weights, so different people view the space along different planes; and it is recomputed per trial from that trial's 20 images, so the same pair placed in two different trials is projected differently. Nothing is fitted here – both fall out of step 1 and the allocation – and the second mirrors a real effect: a participant's placement genuinely depends on which other images sit on screen beside it.
  • Within-subject noise (step iii, estimated) – motor variability in dragging, and genuine confusion about ambiguous pairs. It is what makes a participant fail to reproduce their own arrangement, and it is estimated from the repeat trials: median Spearman ρ=0.243 between a participant's two attempts (10th–90th percentile: 0.084–0.511, n=22).
  • Canvas compression (step iii, not estimated) – the rectangle is a hard boundary, so two images already at opposite corners cannot be pushed further apart no matter how dissimilar they are. This is a genuine constraint of the task rather than a modelling choice: it applies identically to real participants and to simulated ones, and it is not something we could estimate away. Its main consequence is that distances at the top of the range are compressed relative to the underlying dissimilarity, which shows up as a distinctive signature in the noise-versus-distance curve.

Each estimated term is inverted separately: we sweep the corresponding model parameter until the simulated cohort reproduces the empirical observable. Per-subject noise magnitudes are drawn from a lognormal population (σ=0.35, median scale 0.30 of canvas width) rather than held constant, because real participants differ substantially in how careful they are. Whether the inversion succeeded is assessed in Are the Simulated Participants Realistic?.

What the Model Does Not Capture

Stated so the results are read with the right scepticism. The model has no fatigue, practice or order effects: its final trial is drawn from the same distribution as its first. It has no image-specific difficulty – noise magnitude varies by participant, not by stimulus, so an intrinsically confusing image is no harder for it than any other. Catch-trials are not simulated, since they test compliance rather than perception. And the perspective re-weighting assumes participants differ in emphasis across a shared set of dimensions, not that they use genuinely different dimensions from one another.

The Ground Truth

Every simulated participant is a noisy view of one fixed embedding, and every recovered embedding is scored against it. Its quality therefore bounds what any downstream result can mean, which is why it gets its own section.

Building It From the Pilot

The 41 pre-SHINE participants are pooled into one dissimilarity matrix – about a third of the 262,450 pairs are observed by someone – and embedded with weighted SMACOF as described in Generating the Embeddings. Unobserved pairs carry zero weight, so they are neither imputed nor allowed to constrain the solution; their coordinates are whatever the observed pairs imply.

Choosing the Dimensionality

MDS cannot choose d for us, so we measure it: split the pilot in half, embed each half separately, and ask how well the two agree. Agreement that rises with d means the data supports the extra dimensions; agreement that falls means we are fitting noise. The one-SE rule then takes the smallest d not clearly worse than the best.

The rule selects d = 3, at a peak split-half Spearman of ρ = 0.233. That is low: two independent halves of the pilot share roughly 5% of their rank variance, and the curve is flat across d = 3, 4 and 5, so the data cannot really distinguish them. The second measure disagrees with the first, which is itself diagnostic.

Agreement about which pairs are closest keeps improving out to d = 20 while overall agreement is falling. Two measures pointing in opposite directions means the pilot is too small to settle the question. Note that k here counts pairs, not images: the closest 5% of all 262,450 pairs, so k = 13,122.

Does the Embedding Itself Suggest a Dimensionality?

A natural next question is whether the fitted solution shows an elbow of its own, the way a PCA scree plot does. It does not, and the reason is worth stating because it also answers whether fitting a larger embedding would help.

One axis carries 25% of the variance and the remaining seven split the rest almost evenly, from 15% down to 9%. There is no elbow. Fitting at d = 15 and reading its spectrum would not settle it either: SMACOF has no eigenvalues in the sense classical MDS does. It is an iterative stress-minimiser that will use every dimension it is handed, so its coordinates spread variance across the whole space by construction. A flat-tailed spectrum is what the algorithm produces whenever d is larger than the structure requires, which is informative here only in the negative sense: it tells us the space is not dominated by two or three axes, and it tells us nothing about where to stop. The split-half scan above remains the only evidence that bears on the choice, because it is the only one that holds out data.

Why d = 8

We built the ground truth at d = 8 rather than the selected 3, for one reason: a low-dimensional embedding is easier to recover than a high-dimensional one, so planning against d = 3 would flatter the simulation and lead to recruiting too few participants. Raising d makes the recovery problem deliberately harder, so the sample sizes that follow err toward being too large.

Eight specifically is a judgement call, not a derived value, and neither figure above offers an elbow to pick. Split-half agreement declines smoothly from its peak; top-k agreement rises monotonically to d = 20 with steadily shrinking gains and no knee. What can be said for 8 is that it sits comfortably above the scan's selection, inside the range the scan probed, and past the point where each added dimension buys under 0.003 of top-k agreement. Any value in 7–10 would have served the same purpose.

We have no direct evidence of how sensitive the conclusions are to this choice. The sweep varies the dimensionality of the recovered embedding (5 to 18), not of the ground truth, which is fixed at 8 throughout. Re-running stage 2 against a second ground truth is the only way to test it.

Is the Map Any Good?

No simulation is involved in this check. Both bars below come from the same 41 pilot participants, and the comparison is between two quantities:

  • Blue – how closely the ground-truth embedding matches the pooled ratings it was fitted on. This is an in-sample fit statistic, not a validation.
  • Red – the noise ceiling: how well the pilot ratings agree with themselves. Concretely: split the 41 participants at random into two halves, average each half's rating for every pair, correlate the two resulting vectors, and repeat over many random splits. Because each half has only ~20 participants, the result is corrected up to full-sample size with the Spearman–Brown formula (2r / (1 + r)). Red is therefore not a property of the embedding at all – it is the reproducibility of the measurement, and it caps what any model of that measurement could honestly achieve. If two halves of the same participants only agree with each other at ρ = 0.05, then a model agreeing with the pooled data at ρ = 0.5 is matching a pattern that does not replicate.

The two are computed on the same pairs, at each level of the semantic hierarchy, so they are directly comparable bar by bar.

Blue exceeds red by roughly 4× to 20× at the coarse levels. A 725×8 configuration has 5,800 free coordinates and a 31%-observed matrix to constrain them, so it has enough freedom to fit the noise as well as the signal. Exceeding the ceiling is the signature of exactly that.

Semantic Structure

The images carry an independent semantic hierarchy that was never given to the algorithm, so it serves as an external check: if the embedding is meaningful, semantically closer images should sit closer together.

The gradient is monotone for five of six levels, which is reassuring. The deepest level inverts: images sharing a depth-5 leaf sit further apart than same-leaf pairs generally. That cell holds 348 pairs (0.13% of the matrix) and only 100 of them were judged by anyone in the 41-subject set, so its position is largely interpolated. We report it rather than trimming it, and note the consequence: the inversion is inherited by every simulated cohort drawn from this ground truth, so it is never evidence about a particular simulation setting.

How We Evaluate

Six questions, each with its own section, in the order a sceptic would ask them:

  1. Coverage and convergence. Does the allocation arm change what gets seen, does the session-disjointness constraint cost anything, and does SMACOF converge. First because it is the only one that is pure combinatorics: it holds whether or not the participant model is any good.
  2. Are the simulated participants realistic? Nothing downstream of here matters if not.
  3. Screening and recruitment. What screening costs in recruited participants, what it buys in retained quality, and whether that quality reaches the map.
  4. Stability between cohorts. Would a second run of the study produce the same ratings, and the same embedding.
  5. Closest-pair recovery. Do cohorts agree on which pairs are closest, and are those the pairs that are genuinely closest in the ground truth.
  6. Cluster structure. At what granularity, if any, do groupings reproduce.

Coverage and Convergence

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald covers 18–30% more image pairs than random for identical participant effort, the session-disjointness constraint costs under 1% of coverage, and SMACOF converged in 99.9% of fits.

Pair Coverage

The share of the 262,450 possible pairs judged by at least one participant.

The advantage peaks at N=75 and vanishes by N=500, where random already reaches 99.9% and there is nothing left to win.

The absolute level matters as much as the gap. At N=30 the 262,450 pairs receive about 126,000 judgements between them, so the average pair is judged 0.48 times: most pairs are judged by nobody, and those that are judged are judged once. At these sample sizes the difficulty is getting each pair judged at all, and only later becomes a question of measuring it precisely. That is the regime in which planning the allocation helps most.

Allocation Balance and the Cost of the Constraint

The SD of how often each image appears, and the coverage difference between constrained and unconstrained MacDonald. This is pure combinatorics, with no simulated participants and no MDS. The second figure also carries the live study, so the design comparison and the question of where collection has actually reached can be read off one set of axes.

Random allocation is roughly five times less balanced: the SD of how many times each image is shown is 2.74 against 0.51 at N=30. It is also more redundant. SMACOF Convergence

Whether the optimiser reached a solution rather than exhausting its iteration budget, and the stress it reached. Recall from Generating the Embeddings that convergence requires a single connected component; that held for every cohort.

17,709 of 17,729 fits converged; 20 (0.11%) hit the 1000-iteration cap, spread across the grid rather than concentrated in one cell. Stress runs 0.163–0.432 (mean 0.332), which is high in absolute terms and consistent with embedding a sparse, noisy matrix in 5–18 dimensions.

The Study As Deployed

Everything above averages over the marginalised axes, which answers whether a finding holds across a plausible range but never what our study will get. Pinning every swept variable to the deployed value answers the second question.

At N=50 the deployed configuration reaches 48.1% of pairs, and at N=75 62.6%. Note where that sits in the last column: 48.1 is the bottom of the range the sweep spans at this N, because the range includes the designed arm. The deployed random allocation is the least efficient option on the only axis this section measures.

The other three rows are reassuring in a duller way. Every image is seen by somebody at every sample size, the observed-pair graph is a single component in every draw, and the average pair is judged 0.80 times at N=50 – below one, which is the plain statement that at this scale most pairs are still judged by nobody.

Verdict

The allocation arm does what it was designed to do, and the constraint that makes it deployable is close to free. Convergence is not a concern. Whether the extra coverage buys anything is the subject of the next three sections.

Are the Simulated Participants Realistic?

Bottom line. The model reproduces between-subject disagreement almost exactly and reproduces the shape of within-subject noise without having been fitted to it, but it is 28% too noisy within subject and under-separates fine semantic distinctions.

Every check below now has a third column. The pilot was the only empirical anchor available when the model was built; the deployed study is the thing the model was built to predict, so it is the sterner test. Where the two empirical sources disagree, that is a task-version effect rather than noise: the pilot spread its repeat trials through the session, the deployed task concentrates them in a screening block.

The model is the noisiest of the three on every axis, which is the direction that makes its sample-size recommendations conservative rather than optimistic. The one place it does not merely undershoot but points the wrong way is the last row, discussed below.

Calibration Targets (mean pairwise Spearman ρ, test-retest Spearman ρ)

The two observables the noise model was inverted against: agreement between two participants over the pairs both judged, and agreement between one participant's two arrangements of the same 20 images.

That is the fit as the calibration search left it, before a single sweep cell had run. It is worth checking separately whether the 17,280 cohorts the sweep actually produced realised that fit, since a parameter can be chosen correctly and still be applied wrongly downstream.

They did: the unscreened cohorts centre on the calibrated 0.176, tightly and identically in both arms. So the 28% shortfall against the pilot's 0.243 is a limitation of the calibration, not a bug in the sweep. The consequence is one-directional and worth stating plainly: simulated participants are noisier than real ones, so every sample size this report derives is, if anything, too large rather than too small.

Against the pilot, between-subject agreement is reproduced to within 2% (0.142 against 0.145) and within-subject test-retest is under-reproduced by 28% (0.243 against 0.176), because the inversion ran on a coarse search grid and stopped short.

Against the deployed study both gaps are larger, and both run the same way. Real participants reach a median test-retest of 0.268 and a between-subject agreement of 0.166, so the model undershoots reliability by 34% and agreement by 13%. Part of the reliability gap is selection: the production figure is measured on people who already cleared the ρ > 0 gate, and the pilot had no gate. The comparison is still the right way round for planning, though, and it is now better evidenced – every sample size in this report is conservative, and by more than the pilot suggested.

Distance From Random Assignment

The checks above all ask whether the simulated participants reproduce a particular statistic of the pilot. A blunter question sits underneath them: does either group place images any differently from someone dropping them on the canvas at random? The null is easy to compute exactly – 20 points scattered uniformly on the canvas, with distances divided by the diagonal, which is the same normalisation the deployed task applies. Under task-v5 the simulated distances are divided by their canvas diagonal too, so all three distributions sit on one [0, 1] axis with no rescaling.

The pilot participants sit 8% below chance and the simulated ones sit 10% above it. The pilot direction is the expected one: arranging by similarity means pulling groups of images together, which shortens the average pair. The simulation moves the wrong way.

The cause is the canvas fit. Each simulated arrangement is scaled to fill the canvas before the placement noise is added, so the items spread out to the edges by construction, and the resulting mean pair distance ends up above what uniform scattering would give. Real participants leave the canvas unevenly filled: they cluster what belongs together and leave empty space elsewhere.

The Study As Deployed

Verdict

Good enough to proceed, with one known bias and one known limitation. The virtual participants disagree with each other exactly as much as real ones do, and their noise has the right shape. They are too noisy within subject, which makes every sample-size recommendation conservative, and they under-separate fine semantic distinctions, which means results at the finest granularity understate what real data would give.

What the Deployed Gate Actually Does

The rates above are simulated. The study has now run 84 candidates through the real gate, and the deployed task turns out to have two failure modes where the simulation models only one. A candidate can fail the gate in-task, on the screening block, and be paid the reduced rate. Or they can clear the gate, be paid in full, and then fail the same rule on their experimental block – a case the deployed task cannot see, because it evaluates once and never revisits.

Three things stand out, and none of them is what the simulation predicted.

  • The real gate rejects far fewer than modelled. 6 of 84 candidates failed in-task, a 7% rejection rate against the simulated 29% at the same threshold. The model treats participants as noisy but always engaged, and it is calibrated on the pilot's reliability, which we now know understates the deployed cohort's. Both push the simulated rejection rate up.
  • False positives are the larger group. 11 of the 78 who cleared the gate went on to fail it later, so more people degraded after screening than were caught by it. The simulated counterpart of this quantity is about 10% at the same threshold, so the model gets the order of magnitude right here even though it misses the rejection rate badly.
  • Half the failures are for a reason the simulation cannot represent at all. Of the 17 failures, 10 tripped the reliability criterion, 6 the move-ratio criterion and 1 the arrangement-spread criterion. Every simulated subject arranges every image in every trial, so a move-ratio failure is not something the model can produce. That share of the real failure rate is invisible to every screening number elsewhere in this report.

Does a Cleaner Cohort Buy a Better Map?

The table above shows screening does what it claims: retained participants really are more self-consistent. That is not the same as the map being better, and the screening threshold is the one swept axis that varies participant quality rather than quantity, so it is the only handle this simulation has on the question. Holding N = 50 fixed and letting only the threshold move isolates it.

A great deal. Doubling the retained cohort's test-retest reliability, from 0.154 to 0.340, lifts every measure by far more than the spread across the remaining swept settings. This is the largest effect anywhere in the sweep, several times the size of the allocation-arm difference.

Verdict

One caveat runs through all of it. This simulation models participants as noisy, never as absent: no bots, no one who drags two images and quits, no one who mistakes the task entirely. Those are exactly the cases screening exists to catch and the ones the screening block removes most cheaply, so the real pass rate will be lower than the simulated one and the real benefit higher. Both errors point the same way, which is why these numbers should be read as a floor rather than an estimate.

Stability Between Cohorts

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald produces noisier raw ratings but more reproducible embeddings than random, at every sample size. The reversal is the central result of the allocation comparison.

Raw Ratings (Spearman ρ)

The Spearman correlation between two independent cohorts' pooled distance matrices, before any embedding is fitted.

Random wins at every N below 500. Both arms make the same number of judgements; spreading them over more distinct pairs means fewer observations each, so per-pair means are noisier. This is the same fact as the coverage gain, seen from the other side.

Recovered Embeddings (Spearman ρ)

The Spearman correlation between the distance matrices of two embeddings, each fitted to an independent cohort.

The ordering reverses, and constrained-MacDonald leads at every N. Weighted SMACOF uses the observation counts, so a pair measured once still constrains the solution, just more gently. Constraining more of the space beats constraining less of it more precisely.

Recovered Embeddings (Procrustes m²)

The residual sum of squares after optimally translating, rotating and scaling one cohort's configuration onto the other's. Lower is better. It asks whether the two recovered spaces are the same shape, which the Spearman does not.

Same direction as the Spearman. Both measures agreeing matters, because they can disagree: one compares the rank order of distances, the other the shape of the recovered space after optimal alignment.

Is the Difference Real?

The gaps above are small next to the spread across settings, so they need a test rather than an eyeball. Both arms are evaluated at identical settings, so the comparison is paired within each combination of softness, screening threshold, dispersion and embedding dimensionality, which removes the between-setting variance that is not what is being asked about.

Every measure favours the planned allocation, in 96–99% of settings, with Cohen's dz between 1.4 and 2.2. The one exception is the raw ratings, where random wins just as consistently. Note that the differences are small but extremely reliable: the effect sizes are large because the paired differences barely vary, not because the gaps themselves are large.

Closest-Pair Recovery

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald wins at every sample size on every measure, but absolute recovery of the closest pairs remains poor: only half of the ground truth's closest pairs are found even at N=500.

Both measures in this section depend on a cut: how many pairs count as "closest". k is a count of pairs, not of images. The dataset has 262,450 pairs, so the top 1% is k = 2,624 pairs, the top 5% is 13,122, and the top 10% is 26,245. A tighter cut is a harder question and a more relevant one, since the study is ultimately about the nearest neighbourhood of each image. Each figure below is split into panels by cut, so the sensitivity to that choice is visible rather than buried.

Agreement Between Cohorts (top-k Jaccard)

The overlap between the sets of closest pairs identified by two independent cohorts. It asks for reproducibility without reference to truth.

Agreement depends heavily on how tight the cut is. At N=50 two cohorts overlap on 0.088 of the top 5% of pairs (designed arm; 0.083 random), 0.150 of the top 10%, and 0.299 of the top 25%. Even at N=500 the tightest cut only reaches 0.324. The looser the neighbourhood, the more reproducible it is, which is the opposite of what the study needs: the top 5% is closest to the question we are asking, and it is the panel that reproduces worst.

Recall Against the Ground Truth (recall@k)

Take the k closest pairs in the ground truth, take the k closest pairs in a recovered embedding, and ask what share of the first set appears in the second. Because both sets have the same size, recall and precision are identical here, so one number carries it.

The same pattern, against truth rather than against another cohort. At N=50 the designed arm recovers 0.093 of the ground truth's top 1% of pairs, 0.256 of the top 5% and 0.363 of the top 10% (random trails by 0.005–0.010 throughout). At N=500 those become 0.302, 0.541 and 0.648. Every panel is still climbing at the top of the grid, so none of them locates a required N; and at the tightest cut, the one the stimulus construction actually depends on, five hundred participants still miss seven of every ten pairs.

Discrimination Against the Ground Truth (AUC and d′)

AUC is the probability that a truly-close pair is ranked closer than a truly-far one; d′ is the standardised separation between the two distributions. Both ask whether the embedding separates close from far, rather than whether it identifies the exact closest pairs.

AUC is high and rises with N (0.839 at N=50, 0.958 at N=500), and d′ roughly doubles from 1.08 to 2.05 across that range.

The contrast with recall is worth unpicking, because the two sound like the same question. AUC is an average over all pairs: pick a genuinely-close pair and a genuinely-far one at random, and AUC is the probability the embedding ranks them the right way round. Most such comparisons are easy, since most pairs are obviously unrelated. Recall@k is a hard cut at the very top of the ranking, where the competitors are all near-identical images and their order is decided by noise. An embedding can therefore rank close-versus-far correctly 96% of the time and still get the top-k membership half wrong, because being roughly right about everything does not mean being exactly right about the extreme.

This is the table to read if the study's purpose is choosing which images are too similar to pair. At N=50 the deployed configuration recovers 22.9% of the ground truth's closest 5% of pairs, rising to 26.6% at N=75, and two independent cohorts of that size agree on only 7.3% of their own closest pairs, or 9.0% at N=75.

Discrimination is much healthier than overlap: AUC of 0.82 and d′ of 1.00 mean the recovered space does separate close pairs from far ones perfectly respectably. The gap between those two readings is the whole finding of this section, and the deployed configuration does not escape it: the space knows which pairs are closer, but not reliably enough to name the same top 5% twice.

Verdict

Coarse discrimination is solved; fine identification is not. Since the downstream use is excluding confusable pairs, and that decision needs the closest pairs specifically, this is the result that most constrains what the dataset can support. It is also why required N remains unanswered: no curve here has plateaued at N=500.

Cluster Structure

Bottom line. No granularity finer than k=2 reproduces across cohorts. Two independent methods agree, so the practical rule should be a distance threshold rather than cluster membership.

Every figure in this section shades k ≥ 150 in red. At 725 images that is fewer than five images per cluster, which is the granularity the pilot supports least: within same-leaf pairs the raw ratings agree with themselves at about 0.05 (Is the Map Any Good?). Results inside the band describe the embedding's smoothness as much as anything recoverable, so they are shown but should not be leaned on.

Partition Agreement (normalised VI and ARI)

Variation of Information measures the information lost moving between two partitions, normalised to [0,1] and lower-is-better; the adjusted Rand index measures pair-wise co-assignment agreement, chance-corrected and higher-is-better. Both compare two cohorts clustered independently at the same k.

Agreement is high at k=2 (VI 0.074) and decays monotonically: 0.257 at k=5, 0.500 at k=20. ARI tells the same story on a chance-corrected scale. The two allocation arms track each other closely, with the planned arm marginally ahead at every k, so the negative result below is a property of the space rather than of how the images were handed out. Average linkage outperforms ward and complete throughout, as expected from it assuming least about cluster shape; only average is plotted, for legibility.

Separation and Granularity (cross-cohort silhouette, k*)

The cross-cohort silhouette applies one cohort's cluster labels to another cohort's geometry and asks whether the clusters are still separated there. Above zero they are; below zero, points sit closer to a neighbouring cluster than their own. k* is the granularity selected by the one-SE rule.

The curve crosses zero at about k=12 and is negative beyond it, for both arms alike. Across all 5,184 configurations the selected granularity was k* = 2, without a single exception.

That was selected two ways, and the agreement is meaningful because the two rules reward opposite things. The VI rule asks only for reproducibility, and a coarse cut is trivially reproducible, so on its own it is biased toward small k. The silhouette rule asks for separation, which a too-coarse cut destroys by merging genuinely distinct groups, so it is biased toward larger k. On data with real structure at k=5 the first rule would say 2 and the second 5. Here both say 2, which is harder to dismiss than either alone.

Whole-Tree Agreement (Baker's γ, cophenetic correlation)

Both measures avoid choosing a k by comparing the dendrograms themselves.

  • Baker's γ is the rank correlation between two trees over all pairs of images, where each pair is scored by how far up the tree they are first joined. It asks whether two cohorts merge things in the same order, at every scale at once.
  • The cophenetic correlation compares a single tree against the distances it was built from: it asks how much of a cohort's own geometry survives being forced into a hierarchy.

The second sets the terms for the first. If a tree is a poor summary of its own data, then two such trees agreeing tells us they distort in similar ways, not that they found the same structure.

Baker's γ reaches 0.611 for average linkage (0.579 ward, 0.471 complete), with cophenetic correlations of 0.542, 0.529 and 0.498. Moderate agreement on gross merge order, consistent with structure existing at the coarsest scale only.

Density-Based Structure (HDBSCAN noise fraction, Cohen's κ)

Unlike agglomerative clustering, HDBSCAN may leave an image unassigned to any cluster, labelling it “noise”. The noise fraction is the share it declines to cluster; Cohen's κ is the chance-corrected agreement between two cohorts about which images those are.

At min_cluster_size=5, 61% of images are left unclustered and cohorts agree on which at κ = 0.20. At min_cluster_size=2 it finds 171 clusters that two cohorts agree on at ARI 0.05. No setting both covers the image set and reproduces. The two arms are again indistinguishable.

Nor does the picture improve with sample size: the share of unclustered images is essentially flat from N=30 to N=500. Whatever is preventing these images from grouping is not a shortage of participants in the simulated study.

The Study As Deployed

A two-way split reproduces moderately at the deployed configuration (ARI 0.55 at N=50, 0.65 at N=75). Eight clusters do not: ARI falls to 0.24, and normalised VI of 2.99 is close to the value two unrelated partitions would give. HDBSCAN leaves 65% of images unclustered at N=50 and the two cohorts agree on which ones at κ = 0.09, which is barely above chance.

The ranges show this is not an artefact of the deployed choice. Even the best cell in the sweep reaches only ARI 0.44 at k=8, so no configuration available to us recovers fine-grained groupings.

Verdict

The perceptual space behaves as a continuum, not a set of clumps. Agglomerative clustering says fine partitions do not reproduce; HDBSCAN says most images were never in a group to begin with. Part of this is the ground truth's sparsity (Is the Map Any Good?) rather than the images, so it should be revisited once the full study supplies a better map.

Conclusions

What the Simulation Settles

  1. The simulated participants are adequate but conservative. They are 28% noisier than real ones within subject, so recommendations err toward too many participants rather than too few. They also over-disperse: they place images slightly further apart than random assignment would, where real participants cluster them closer.
  2. Screen, and the deployed ρ > 0 is a reasonable place to do it. Participant quality is the largest single lever in the sweep: at N=50, screening at ρ > 0.2 raises cohort agreement 57% and closest-pair agreement 73%, about four times the allocation effect. It is affordable because a screened-out participant is paid only for the screening block, so rejecting 77% of candidates doubles rather than quadruples the bill. Tightening to ρ > 0.1 is defensible; going beyond it is not.
  3. N = 50–75 buys a usable coarse map and no fine structure. Two independent cohorts of this size agree at Spearman ρ = 0.505 (N=50) and 0.582 (N=75), with Procrustes m² of 0.759 and 0.688 – moderate reproducibility that keeps improving, not a plateau. The same cohorts reproduce a two-way split well (ARI 0.60 and 0.70 at k=2) and everything finer poorly: ARI falls to about 0.30 by k=8 and to 0.18 and 0.24 by k=50. HDBSCAN leaves 64% and 60% of images unclustered, agreeing on which ones at κ = 0.13 and 0.19. Going from 50 to 75 participants helps everywhere and settles nothing: it is a shift along a smooth curve, not a threshold being crossed.
  4. Adopt constrained-MacDonald allocation. It covers 18–30% more pairs for identical effort, produces more reproducible embeddings at every N on both Spearman ρ and Procrustes m², recovers the ground truth better on every measure, and the constraint that makes it deployable costs 0.61% of coverage.
  5. Do not build the analysis on cluster membership. Use a distance threshold. No granularity finer than k=2 survives resampling, and two independent methods agree. Caveat: this may be a statement about the pilot rather than about the images. The ground truth is fitted to 41 participants and exceeds its own data's reliability ceiling, so its fine structure is partly fitted noise, and clusters that are not really there cannot reproduce. A continuum of images and a hierarchy too weakly measured to resolve would look identical here.

What Remains Open

  1. Required Ntotal. Unanswered. Recall@k and AUC are still climbing at N=500, so the grid never reached a plateau. What the run does establish is where the allocation advantage lives (N=50–75) and that N=50–75 yields embeddings that reproduce moderately (ρ ≈ 0.51–0.59) while identifying under a quarter of the closest pairs.
  2. How much of the clustering result is the ground truth rather than the images. The d=8 embedding exceeds its own data's reliability ceiling by 4–20×, so its fine structure is partly fitted noise. Distinguishing "these images form a continuum" from "41 participants cannot resolve the groups" requires the full study.
  3. Whether real participants would cluster better. The model under-separates fine semantic distinctions relative to the pilot, so the true granularity ceiling may sit above k=2. The direction of that bias is known; its magnitude is not.

What We Are Changing Mid-Collection

These simulations were run to decide how to collect the remaining half of the study, so the decisions belong here rather than in a follow-up. Each row states what changes, where the boundary is recoverable in the data, and why it cannot bias the registered analyses.

The general argument for all of them: SHINE variant is assigned by a hash of the Prolific ID, so it is independent of when a participant is run. Any change applied at a point in time therefore lands on both cohorts equally and enters the analysis as a block factor, not a confound. The task_version column on every row marks the boundary.

Allocation Switch the remaining participants from random assignment to the constrained-MacDonald design, conditioned on the pairs already covered so that the remaining trials fill the gaps the random half left.
