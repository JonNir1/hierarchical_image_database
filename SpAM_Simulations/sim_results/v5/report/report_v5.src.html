Background

The Project

We have 725 photographs of objects. We want to generate a similarity map over the image, to determine which images are too similar to be presented together in a visual search task. To create the map, we use Prolific to collect similarity ratings from online raters.
As a secondary goal, we want to check if the image preprocessing pipeline that is commonly applied on EEG/MRI experiments using the SHINE toolkit affects the perceptual similarity scores of the processed images.

The SpAM Task

The dataset consists of 725 images, which make 262,450 possible pairs. No single subject can rate that many pairs. Instead, we use the Spatial Arrangement Method: participants are presented with a subset of k images, and they are asked to place each image within a 2D canvas based on their similarity – similar images should be closer together, and dissimilar images are placed far apart. This way, perceptual similarity is inferred from the Euclidean distance between pairs of images, which can be collected much faster than the classical pairwise ratings using a Likert scale. A recent study (Robbins et al., 2025, experiment 2) reported correlations between the two methods ranging from r = 0.04 to 0.40 depending on category.

Image-to-Trial Allocation

With t=18 trials and k=20 unique images per trial, each (retained) participant covers 360 of the 725 images, and produces ½t·k(k-1) = 3420 distinct ratings (not including the repeated trials), which are 1.3% of all possible ratings. Importantly, the images are allocated randomly across subjects, which could lead to some images being over sampled while others are rarely seen, and many image-pairs never being judged at all. An alternative allocation method, introduced by MacDonald et al. (2020), guarantees an almost-optimally balanced allocation. However, it does not use strictly disjoint subsets of images across trials for the same participant – a criterion we require due to our repeatability analysis (repeated trials). Therefore, we created a constrained version of the MacDonald allocation algorithm, which forfeits some of the allocation-balance in order to guarantee a participant only sees images within a single context (i.e., no image appears in more than a single trial, except for repeated trials where the whole context is repeated).
While it is expected that the MacDonald algorithm (and our constrained version) would outperform the random assignment in pair-coverage and allocation balance, it is unclear whether the wider coverage translates to a worthwhile improvement in embedding stability, generalizability, or granularity.

Our Experiment

We collected data from Ntotal participants using Prolific. Each participant performed t=18 SpAM trials, each with k=20 unique images. Four repeat-trials – where participants needed to re-arrange a set of images they had already seen – were interleaved throughout the experiment. Those repeated measurements are used to assess participants' repeatability. Additionally, three catch-trials were presented randomly, requiring the participant to perform a slight variation of the task (e.g. "move all images to the bottom-right corner").
The first 8 trials (6 original, 2 repeats) were used to screen out subjects who do not perform the task: they move too-few items, their arrangement is too concentrated, or they fail to reproduce their own arrangements (Spearman R < 0).

Deriving the Perceptual Map

Generating the Embeddings

Each participant contributes a sparse set of pairwise distances. Pooling them across participants yields a single 725×725 dissimilarity matrix in which most cells remain empty. We turn that matrix into coordinates using Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) – specifically weighted SMACOF, which assigns zero weight to unobserved pairs so they neither constrain the solution nor need to be imputed.
Two properties of MDS matter for everything that follows. First, the dimensionality is an input, not an output: the algorithm embeds the images in however many dimensions it is given and returns a solution for any choice, so d must be selected on separate grounds (see Choosing the Dimensionality). Second, the graph of observed pairs must form a single connected component – if the images split into two subsets with no judged pair bridging them, their relative position is unconstrained and no unique solution exists. Connectivity is necessary but far from sufficient: a barely-connected graph still converges, but to an unstable solution that a second sample would not reproduce. Quantifying that gap is the purpose of Is the Map Any Good?.

Clustering the Embeddings

Grouping the images is the point of the exercise, so we cluster the recovered coordinates two ways, chosen because they fail differently. Agglomerative clustering merges the closest pairs of clusters upward into a dendrogram (we use average, ward and complete linkage), which is then cut at a chosen k. It assigns every image to some cluster, and the resulting partitions support Variation of Information, a true metric on partitions, so two cohorts' groupings can be compared on a scale that obeys the triangle inequality. Its weakness is exactly that completeness: an image resembling nothing else is still forced into a group. HDBSCAN is density-based and governed by min_cluster_size rather than by k; it discovers how many clusters there are and may leave an image unassigned, labelling it noise. That answers the question agglomerative clustering structurally cannot – "does this image belong to any group at all?" – which for stimulus construction is the useful answer, since an image resembling nothing else is the safest one to use. Its noise label breaks the metric properties, so it is run descriptively and never enters the VI chain.

Why Run These Simulations?

Three questions, none of which can be answered from the pilot alone, and all of which have to be settled before committing to a full data collection.

  1. Required Ntotal. How many participants must be recruited in order to retain N who pass screening and yield sufficiently stable embeddings.
  2. Sensitivity analysis at N = 50 and 75. Failing a definitive answer to (1), whether those specific sample sizes give embeddings stable and generalizable enough for the downstream claims we intend to make.
  3. Allocation-arm comparison. Random versus constrained-MacDonald, judged both on pair coverage and balance, and on the same stability and generalizability criteria as (2). Wider coverage is expected; whether it buys better embeddings is not.

From Embedding to Arrangement

A simulated participant is generated in three steps:

  1. Perspective – the d-dimensional ground-truth coordinates are re-weighted per subject, so different people effectively attend to different dimensions. The weights are drawn once per subject and stay fixed for their whole session.
  2. Projection – for each trial, the k=20 selected images are projected onto the two leading principal axes of the re-weighted cloud. This is the same lossy step a real participant performs when forced to express a high-dimensional judgement on a flat screen.
    Note what the re-weighting in step 1 buys here. Inflating a dimension inflates its variance, which promotes it into the top two: a subject weighting dimension 5 heavily sees a plane dominated by dimension 5, while another subject sees a different plane entirely. This is how the population samples all d dimensions rather than everyone viewing the two globally-largest ones. The projection itself involves no randomness – it is fully determined by the subject's weights and by which images share the trial.
  3. Placement – the projected points are fitted into a bounded rectangle and perturbed by placement noise. Distances are then read off the final arrangement exactly as the task records them.

The rectangle matters. Real arrangements are constrained by the canvas edges, and an unbounded model produced per-trial maximum distances of 1.39 on a scale whose ceiling is 1.0 – arrangements no participant could have made.

Noise Estimation

Variability enters the model at each of the three steps above, and the sources are distinct. Two are estimated from the pilot; the other two are structural, and would be present however the model were parameterised. Taking them in step order:

  • Across-subject noise (step i, estimated) – systematic differences in which dimensions a person weighs. This is not random error: each subject is internally consistent, they simply disagree about what matters. It is what makes two careful participants produce different arrangements, and it is estimated from between-subject agreement over the pairs they both judged (mean r=0.142, n=47).
  • Projection variability (step ii, not estimated) – the 2D plane is not fixed. It depends on the subject's weights, so different people view the space along different planes; and it is recomputed per trial from that trial's 20 images, so the same pair placed in two different trials is projected differently. Nothing is fitted here – both fall out of step 1 and the allocation – and the second mirrors a real effect: a participant's placement genuinely depends on which other images sit on screen beside it.
  • Within-subject noise (step iii, estimated) – motor variability in dragging, and genuine confusion about ambiguous pairs. It is what makes a participant fail to reproduce their own arrangement, and it is estimated from the repeat trials: median Spearman ρ=0.243 between a participant's two attempts (10th–90th percentile: 0.084–0.511, n=22).
  • Canvas compression (step iii, not estimated) – the rectangle is a hard boundary, so two images already at opposite corners cannot be pushed further apart no matter how dissimilar they are. This is a genuine constraint of the task rather than a modelling choice: it applies identically to real participants and to simulated ones, and it is not something we could estimate away. Its main consequence is that distances at the top of the range are compressed relative to the underlying dissimilarity, which shows up as a distinctive signature in the noise-versus-distance curve.

Each estimated term is inverted separately: we sweep the corresponding model parameter until the simulated cohort reproduces the empirical observable. Per-subject noise magnitudes are drawn from a lognormal population (σ=0.35, median scale 0.30 of canvas width) rather than held constant, because real participants differ substantially in how careful they are. Whether the inversion succeeded is assessed in Are the Simulated Participants Realistic?.

What the Model Does Not Capture

Stated so the results are read with the right scepticism. The model has no fatigue, practice or order effects: its final trial is drawn from the same distribution as its first. It has no image-specific difficulty – noise magnitude varies by participant, not by stimulus, so an intrinsically confusing image is no harder for it than any other. Catch-trials are not simulated, since they test compliance rather than perception. And the perspective re-weighting assumes participants differ in emphasis across a shared set of dimensions, not that they use genuinely different dimensions from one another.

The Ground Truth

Every simulated participant is a noisy view of one fixed embedding, and every recovered embedding is scored against it. Its quality therefore bounds what any downstream result can mean, which is why it gets its own section.

Building It From the Pilot

The 41 pre-SHINE participants are pooled into one dissimilarity matrix – about a third of the 262,450 pairs are observed by someone – and embedded with weighted SMACOF as described in Generating the Embeddings. Unobserved pairs carry zero weight, so they are neither imputed nor allowed to constrain the solution; their coordinates are whatever the observed pairs imply.

Choosing the Dimensionality

MDS cannot choose d for us, so we measure it: split the pilot in half, embed each half separately, and ask how well the two agree. Agreement that rises with d means the data supports the extra dimensions; agreement that falls means we are fitting noise. The one-SE rule then takes the smallest d not clearly worse than the best.

The rule selects d = 3, at a peak split-half Spearman of ρ = 0.233. That is low: two independent halves of the pilot share roughly 5% of their rank variance, and the curve is flat across d = 3, 4 and 5, so the data cannot really distinguish them. The second measure disagrees with the first, which is itself diagnostic.

Agreement about which pairs are closest keeps improving out to d = 20 while overall agreement is falling. Two measures pointing in opposite directions means the pilot is too small to settle the question.

We therefore built the ground truth at d = 8, not 3. A low-dimensional embedding is easier to recover than a high-dimensional one, so planning the full study against d = 3 would flatter the simulation and lead to recruiting too few participants. Using d = 8 makes the recovery problem deliberately harder, so the sample sizes that follow err toward being too large rather than too small.

Is the Map Any Good?

The embedding is fitted on the pilot, so it cannot legitimately agree with the pilot better than the pilot agrees with itself. We compare two quantities per semantic level: the embedding's agreement with the pooled ratings, and the half-split reliability of those ratings, which is the ceiling.

The embedding exceeds its own ceiling by roughly 4× to 20× at the coarse levels. That is the signature of a 725×8 configuration with more freedom than a 31%-observed matrix can constrain: it is reproducing variance the data cannot reproduce in itself.

What this does and does not undermine. Fine-grained conclusions, especially about clusters of very similar images, are partly a statement about having 41 pilot participants rather than about perception. The allocation comparison is unaffected: it asks which arm covers the pair space better, and both arms are scored against the identical ground truth.

Semantic Structure

The images carry an independent semantic hierarchy that was never given to the algorithm, so it serves as an external check: if the embedding is meaningful, semantically closer images should sit closer together.

The gradient is monotone for five of six levels, which is reassuring. The deepest level inverts: images sharing a depth-5 leaf sit further apart than same-leaf pairs generally. That cell holds 348 pairs (0.13% of the matrix) and only 100 of them were judged by anyone in the 41-subject set, so its position is largely interpolated. We report it rather than trimming it, and note the consequence: the inversion is inherited by every simulated cohort drawn from this ground truth, so it is never evidence about a particular simulation setting.

How We Evaluate

Five questions, each with its own section, in the order a sceptic would ask them:

  1. Are the simulated participants realistic? Nothing downstream matters if not.
  2. Coverage and convergence. Does the allocation arm change what gets seen, does the session-disjointness constraint cost anything, and does SMACOF converge.
  3. Stability between cohorts. Would a second run of the study produce the same ratings, and the same embedding.
  4. Closest-pair recovery. Do cohorts agree on which pairs are closest, and are those the pairs that are genuinely closest in the ground truth.
  5. Cluster structure. At what granularity, if any, do groupings reproduce.

Are the Simulated Participants Realistic?

Bottom line. The model reproduces between-subject disagreement almost exactly and reproduces the shape of within-subject noise without having been fitted to it, but it is 28% too noisy within subject and under-separates fine semantic distinctions.

Calibration Targets (mean pairwise Spearman ρ, test-retest Spearman ρ)

The two observables the noise model was inverted against: agreement between two participants over the pairs both judged, and agreement between one participant's two arrangements of the same 20 images.

Between-subject agreement is reproduced to within 2% (0.142 against 0.145). Within-subject test-retest is under-reproduced by 28% (0.243 against 0.176) because the inversion ran on a coarse search grid and stopped short. That biases in the safe direction, since a noisier virtual participant makes recovery harder than reality, but it is a defect rather than a design choice.

Reliability Against Distance (binned RMSE)

For every pair a participant judged twice, the RMSE between the two placements, binned by how far apart they placed it. This was not fitted, which is what gives it force.

Both curves rise off a floor and then fall. The falling right flank requires a bounded canvas, since two images already at opposite corners cannot be pushed further apart. The model reproduces it (drop from peak 0.27 against the pilot's 0.37). The mismatch is on the left: the model never becomes as quiet as real participants on obviously-similar pairs.

Semantic Gradient Against the Pilot

Mean simulated distance per semantic level, relative to unrelated pairs, compared with the same quantity in the pilot.

The ordering is correct but compressed: same-leaf pairs sit at ~0.71 of the unrelated distance in simulation against ~0.28 in the pilot. Part is inherited from the ground truth (Is the Map Any Good?) and part from the noise model. This check was scored on one configuration per arm, so it establishes that the gradient reproduces somewhere in the parameter space rather than throughout it.

Verdict

Good enough to proceed, with one known bias and one known limitation. The virtual participants disagree with each other exactly as much as real ones do, and their noise has the right shape. They are too noisy within subject, which makes every sample-size recommendation conservative, and they under-separate fine semantic distinctions, which means results at the finest granularity understate what real data would give.

Coverage and Convergence

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald covers 18–30% more image pairs than random for identical participant effort, the session-disjointness constraint costs under 1% of coverage, and SMACOF converged in 99.9% of fits.

Pair Coverage

The share of the 262,450 possible pairs judged by at least one participant.

Coverage rises from 32.5% to 38.4% at N=30, 48.1% to 60.3% at N=50, and 62.6% to 81.3% at N=75. The advantage peaks at N=75 and vanishes by N=500, where random already reaches 99.9% and there is nothing left to win. Note the scale of the problem at small N: at 30 participants the average pair is judged 0.48 times, so the binding constraint is touching each pair at all rather than measuring it well.

Allocation Balance and the Cost of the Constraint

The SD of how often each image appears, and the coverage difference between constrained and unconstrained MacDonald. This is pure combinatorics, with no simulated participants and no MDS.

Random allocation is roughly five times less balanced (SD 2.74 against 0.51 at N=30) and wastes far more effort on already-covered pairs (35.9% against 16.8% at N=75). The disjointness constraint costs 0.61% of coverage relative at N=50 (60.31% against 60.68%), which is the result that makes the deployable variant usable.

SMACOF Convergence

Whether the optimiser reached a solution rather than exhausting its iteration budget, and the stress it reached. Recall from Generating the Embeddings that convergence requires a single connected component; that held for every cohort.

17,709 of 17,729 fits converged; 20 (0.11%) hit the 1000-iteration cap, spread across the grid rather than concentrated in one cell. (The store holds 17,729 rows against a designed grid of 17,280: a resumed run appended 449 duplicate fits, which are de-duplicated before any metric is computed.) Stress runs 0.163–0.432 (mean 0.332), which is high in absolute terms and consistent with embedding a sparse, noisy matrix in 5–18 dimensions.

Verdict

The allocation arm does what it was designed to do, and the constraint that makes it deployable is close to free. Convergence is not a concern. Whether the extra coverage buys anything is the subject of the next three sections.

Stability Between Cohorts

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald produces noisier raw ratings but more reproducible embeddings than random, at every sample size. The reversal is the central result of the allocation comparison.

Raw Ratings (Spearman ρ)

The Spearman correlation between two independent cohorts' pooled distance matrices, before any embedding is fitted.

Random wins at every N below 500 (0.143 against 0.126 at N=50). Both arms make the same number of judgements; spreading them over more distinct pairs means fewer observations each, so per-pair means are noisier. This is the same fact as the coverage gain, seen from the other side.

Recovered Embeddings (Spearman ρ)

The Spearman correlation between the distance matrices of two embeddings, each fitted to an independent cohort.

The ordering reverses: 0.514 against 0.495 at N=50, and constrained-MacDonald leads at every N. Weighted SMACOF uses the observation counts, so a pair measured once still constrains the solution, just more gently. Constraining more of the space beats constraining less of it more precisely.

Recovered Embeddings (Procrustes m²)

The residual sum of squares after optimally translating, rotating and scaling one cohort's configuration onto the other's. Lower is better. It asks whether the two recovered spaces are the same shape, which the Spearman does not.

Same direction: 0.752 against 0.766 at N=50. Both measures agreeing matters, because they can disagree.

Verdict

The pre-MDS correlation is the input to the method, not its output, and the design decision should not rest on it. On the quantity that matters, the allocation arm wins throughout. At N=50, paired within every sensitivity setting, the embedding-level advantage holds in 96–99% of settings with Cohen's dz between 1.4 and 2.2.

Closest-Pair Recovery

Bottom line. Constrained-MacDonald wins at every sample size on every measure, but absolute recovery of the closest pairs remains poor: only half of the ground truth's closest pairs are found even at N=500.

Agreement Between Cohorts (top-k Jaccard)

The overlap between the sets of closest pairs identified by two independent cohorts. It asks for reproducibility without reference to truth.

0.179 against 0.172 at N=50, rising to 0.452 against 0.437 at N=500. Two cohorts agree on under a fifth of their closest pairs in the deployable range.

Recall Against the Ground Truth (recall@k)

The share of the ground truth's genuinely closest pairs that a recovered embedding also ranks closest.

0.237 against 0.229 at N=50, reaching only 0.497 at N=500. This curve is still climbing at the top of the grid.

Discrimination Against the Ground Truth (AUC and d′)

AUC is the probability that a truly-close pair is ranked closer than a truly-far one; d′ is the standardised separation between the two distributions. Both ask whether the embedding separates close from far, rather than whether it identifies the exact closest pairs.

AUC is high and rises with N (0.839 against 0.831 at N=50; 0.958 at N=500), and d′ roughly doubles from 1.08 to 2.05 across that range. The contrast with recall is the point: the embedding reliably separates close pairs from far ones while still failing to pin down which pairs are closest.

Verdict

Coarse discrimination is solved; fine identification is not. Since the downstream use is excluding confusable pairs, and that decision needs the closest pairs specifically, this is the result that most constrains what the dataset can support. It is also why required N remains unanswered: no curve here has plateaued at N=500.

Cluster Structure

Bottom line. No granularity finer than k=2 reproduces across cohorts. Two independent methods agree, so the practical rule should be a distance threshold rather than cluster membership.

Partition Agreement (normalised VI and ARI)

Variation of Information measures the information lost moving between two partitions, normalised to [0,1] and lower-is-better; the adjusted Rand index measures pair-wise co-assignment agreement, chance-corrected and higher-is-better. Both compare two cohorts clustered independently at the same k.

Agreement is high at k=2 (VI 0.074 for average linkage) and decays monotonically: 0.257 at k=5, 0.500 at k=20. ARI tells the same story on a chance-corrected scale. Average linkage outperforms ward and complete throughout, as expected from it assuming least about cluster shape.

Separation and Granularity (cross-cohort silhouette, k*)

The cross-cohort silhouette applies one cohort's cluster labels to another cohort's geometry and asks whether the clusters are still separated there. Above zero they are; below zero, points sit closer to a neighbouring cluster than their own. k* is the granularity selected by the one-SE rule.

The curve crosses zero at about k=12 and is negative beyond it. Across all 5,184 configurations the selected granularity was k* = 2, on both the VI and silhouette criteria, without a single exception. The two criteria are constructed to disagree where real structure exists, so their unanimity is informative rather than redundant.

Whole-Tree Agreement (Baker's γ, cophenetic correlation)

Baker's γ correlates the merge order of two dendrograms, independent of any cut. The cophenetic correlation measures how faithfully a tree represents its own distance matrix, and qualifies the first: trees that poorly describe their own data agreeing with each other means little.

Baker's γ reaches 0.611 for average linkage (0.579 ward, 0.471 complete), with cophenetic correlations of 0.542, 0.529 and 0.498. Moderate agreement on gross merge order, consistent with structure existing at the coarsest scale only.

Density-Based Structure (HDBSCAN noise fraction, Cohen's κ)

HDBSCAN may leave an image unassigned. The noise fraction is the share it declines to cluster; Cohen's κ is the chance-corrected agreement between two cohorts about which images those are.

At min_cluster_size=5, 61% of images are left unclustered and cohorts agree on which at κ = 0.20. At min_cluster_size=2 it finds 171 clusters that two cohorts agree on at ARI 0.05. No setting both covers the image set and reproduces.

Verdict

The perceptual space behaves as a continuum, not a set of clumps. Agglomerative clustering says fine partitions do not reproduce; HDBSCAN says most images were never in a group to begin with. Part of this is the ground truth's sparsity (Is the Map Any Good?) rather than the images, so it should be revisited once the full study supplies a better map.

Conclusions

What the Simulation Settles

  1. Adopt constrained-MacDonald allocation. It covers 18–30% more pairs for identical effort, produces more reproducible embeddings at every N on both Spearman ρ and Procrustes m², recovers the ground truth better on every measure, and the constraint that makes it deployable costs 0.61% of coverage.
  2. Do not build the analysis on cluster membership. Use a distance threshold. No granularity finer than k=2 survives resampling, and two independent methods agree.
  3. The simulated participants are adequate but conservative. They are 28% noisier than real ones within subject, so recommendations err toward too many participants rather than too few.

What Remains Open

  1. Required Ntotal. Unanswered. Recall@k and AUC are still climbing at N=500, so the grid never reached a plateau. What the run does establish is where the allocation advantage lives (N=50–75) and that N=50–75 yields embeddings that reproduce moderately (ρ ≈ 0.51–0.59) while identifying under a quarter of the closest pairs.
  2. How much of the clustering result is the ground truth rather than the images. The d=8 embedding exceeds its own data's reliability ceiling by 4–20×, so its fine structure is partly fitted noise. Distinguishing "these images form a continuum" from "41 participants cannot resolve the groups" requires the full study.
  3. Whether real participants would cluster better. The model under-separates fine semantic distinctions relative to the pilot, so the true granularity ceiling may sit above k=2. The direction of that bias is known; its magnitude is not.
