sim-v6: choosing how to finish the study

A decision run. Three options, one recommendation, and the first real power estimate for RQ2.

1. Why this run exists

Collection paused at roughly 80 retained subjects, 39 and 39 across the two SHINE cohorts, on a three-way choice about how to spend the remaining budget. All three keep the random allocation arm, which is what is deployed and is not being revisited.

Everything decided so far rested on the v5 sweep, and v5 has four problems for this particular purpose. It never simulated these three cells directly. Its participants were calibrated on 41 pilot subjects running task versions 1.0 to 3.06, whose fitted noise scale is substantially too high for the deployed cohort. Its ground truth exceeds its own data's reliability ceiling, so part of its fine structure is fitted noise. And it has no RQ2 power estimate at all: the numbers produced earlier were an analytic approximation resting on a variance-scaling exponent that could not be justified.

This run is deliberately small, about 3,600 MDS fits against v5's 17,280. It is not a survey and should not be read as one.

2. What differs from v5

  1. Participants are recalibrated on production, all 84 candidates who sat the deployed task rather than the 41 pilot subjects. The fitted noise scale falls from 0.30 to 0.22 and the population's spread from σ = 0.35 to 0.25: the deployed cohort is materially cleaner than the pilot was, which is the single largest reason v5's screening predictions missed.
  2. The ground truth is rebuilt from the pilot plus the eight pre-SHINE production subjects the analysis discards anyway. See section 4.
  3. Retained cohorts exclude false alarms, matching the analysis that would actually be run rather than the task's own one-shot gate.
  4. RQ2 power is measured, by simulating cohorts on deliberately perturbed ground truths.
  5. Within-session drift is swept rather than assumed. See section 5.

Everything else is v5 and is cited rather than repeated. The fitted constants for this run are noise_scale = 0.22, lognormal σ = 0.25, dispersion = 0.15 (swept over 0.0 / 0.15 / 0.30), recorded in calibration/calibration.json.

On production that was 0.22 overridden to 0.30, which alone moved the simulated screening pass rate from 0.749 against an observed 0.929.

4. The rebuilt ground truth

v5's ground truth is fitted on 41 pre-SHINE pilot subjects and its diagnosed weakness is that it exceeds its own data's reliability ceiling: it interpolates where the pair graph is thin. The cure for that is replication, not more dimensions. Eight pre-SHINE production subjects were sitting unused, the ones the deployed gate rejected in-task plus the ones who cleared it and then failed the same rule on their experimental block.

This is not circular. Every one of the eight is excluded from the analysed pool by a rule fixed before this analysis, so no subject who will appear in the study's results contributes to the ground truth its design was chosen against. They are also pre-SHINE, so they judged the same stimulus set the ground truth is a geometry over.

The acceptance test was one-sided by design: the augmented set has larger split-halves and is favoured mechanically, so only a worsening beyond noise would have rejected it. In the event it improved on every diagnostic, and the two that matter most are the last two. The noise ceiling doubled, which is the quantity the old ground truth was overshooting. And the disconnection rate collapsed from 42% to 9%, meaning the pair graph is no longer barely connected, so the split-half estimate is far less exposed to the selection worry that a disconnected-draw filter introduces.

5. On within-session drift

Production shows something v5's model cannot produce: every move-ratio failure in the live data falls in the experimental block and none in the screening block. Participants get worse after the gate has read them, and the deployed design is structurally blind to it. A single scalar multiplying a subject's noise in the second block is the smallest thing that can carry that.

The gate passes at both drift = 1.0 (none) and drift = 1.1 (the value fitted to production's false-alarm rate), so the data does not settle the question. It was swept rather than chosen because the choice is not neutral for the decision: drift lowers retained reliability and raises the false-alarm rate, making every recovery metric worse and every retained subject more expensive, which pushes the answer toward the options that cost more. Picking one value by hand would have tilted a spending decision on an assumption the data cannot support.

It turned out not to matter. Both levels shift every metric down by a similar amount and leave the ordering of the three options identical, which is exactly what sweeping it was meant to establish. Every figure below shows both.

Screening costs money, not coverage. A and B have identical coverage because a rejected candidate is replaced rather than simply dropped, so the retained cohort is N = 50 either way and sees the same number of trials. The entire price of a tighter gate is paid in credits. That is worth stating plainly because the intuition runs the other way.

B is dominated. For 47 more credits, a 7% increase on its 636, option C beats it on every metric here, and adds 14.5 percentage points of coverage that B cannot buy at any price. There is no budget at which B is the right choice: anyone who can afford B can very nearly afford C, and C is better on everything. This is the cleanest result in the run.

That leaves A against C. C costs 397 more credits, 2.4 times A's remaining spend, and returns about 12% more between-cohort agreement, 20% more recall, 32% more top-k Jaccard and 30% more coverage.

7. A model-free check, on the real data

Everything in section 6 comes from a generative model, and a model that has been recalibrated three times deserves a check that does not depend on it. Before this simulation was planned, the same question was put to the production data directly: a split-half analysis comparing the two levers on real subjects, scored on a fixed reference pair set so that the two arms are measured on the same quantity.

LeverChange in split-half agreementCost
Tighten the gate (ρ lever)+11.9%no extra subjects
Add subjects (N lever)+7.9%+65% subjects

The ρ lever beat the N lever on every resample, P(ρ > N) = 1.000. Read naively that favours B, and it appears to contradict section 6.

It does not, and the reason is the fixed pair set. Holding the reference pairs constant is what makes the two arms comparable, but it also holds coverage constant, and coverage is the N lever's main benefit for the embedding. The split-half test therefore measures only what each lever does to the quality of judgements on pairs you already observe, and on that narrow question the ρ lever genuinely wins. The simulation lets coverage move, and coverage is where C's advantage mostly comes from: 62.6% against 48.1%, on a space where 36% of pairs are currently judged by nobody.

So the two analyses agree about the mechanism and differ only in scope. Taken together they say: per subject retained, a cleaner subject is worth more than an extra one; but the study still needs pairs it has never seen, and only N buys those.

8. RQ2 power

RQ2a(ii) asks whether ρ(pre, post) sits below the within-condition ceiling. The null is free: two cohorts drawn from the same ground truth is exactly what the main sweep already produces. The alternative needs cohorts on a ground truth a known distance away, which the perturbation arm supplies. Power is the share of alternative draws below the null's 5th percentile, with no normal approximation and no attenuation formula.

Three things follow, and the qualifications matter as much as the numbers.

  • A large SHINE effect is already detectable. At ρ = 0.90 every option reaches at least 0.93. If SHINE substantially rearranges the space, RQ2 is not a reason to spend anything.
  • A small effect is out of reach for all three. At ρ ≥ 0.98 no option clears 0.33. No amount of money on the table here buys that.
  • C buys the middle band, on a noisy estimate. The 0.87 at ρ = 0.95 rests on 30 draws, a binomial SE of 0.061, so a 95% interval of roughly 0.75 to 0.99. C's margin over A there is about two standard errors: suggestive, not established.

The effect is isotropic by construction. The perturbation adds noise to every coordinate equally and makes no claim about where SHINE acts. If it acts selectively on sensory dimensions, which is precisely what RQ3 supposes, this estimate is optimistic or pessimistic depending on how much of the distance variance those dimensions carry. A sensory-weighted variant is buildable from the sens_pre / sens_post RDMs already in analysis/rdms/, and it is the natural follow-up.

9. Recommendation

Rule out B. This is not a judgement call. C costs 47 more credits and is better on every measured quantity, including 14.5 percentage points of coverage B cannot buy at all. B should not be chosen at any budget that permits it.

Between A and C, take C if the budget allows it. 397 additional credits buy roughly 12% more between-cohort agreement, 20% more recall, 32% more top-k Jaccard, and 30% more coverage, plus the only meaningful improvement in RQ2 power available. The coverage gain is the part that cannot be recovered later: the gate can be tightened at any time, but pairs no one judged stay unjudged, and 36% of the space is currently in that state.

A is not a bad outcome. If the budget is genuinely tight, A still detects a large SHINE effect with power 0.93, still yields a between-cohort agreement of 0.64, and leaves 397 credits for something else. The honest framing is that C is better value per credit than B, not that A is unusable.

What would change this recommendation. If the sensory-weighted RQ2 variant showed that SHINE acts on dimensions carrying little distance variance, the power column would shift and the case for C would rest on coverage alone. If a further 30 subjects were collected and the ρ-lever advantage of section 7 grew rather than held, the balance between the levers would need revisiting. Neither is expensive to check, and both are cheaper than the difference between A and C.

10. What this run cannot settle

  • The rebuilt ground truth is still mostly the pilot, 49 subjects rather than 41, and the eight added are the weakest available. Every diagnostic improved, but the acceptance test was one-sided and a gain of this size is weak evidence rather than vindication.
  • The RQ2 effect is isotropic, as set out in section 8.
  • Drift is a description, not a mechanism. Fatigue, disengagement and strategy change are indistinguishable here, and the scalar claims only a direction and a rough size.
  • The false-alarm rate under drift 1.1 is a fitted target, not a prediction, and must not be read as corroboration of the model at that setting.
  • Both the model and the model-free check share one dataset. They are not independent evidence in the strict sense; they are two different questions asked of the same 84 people.
